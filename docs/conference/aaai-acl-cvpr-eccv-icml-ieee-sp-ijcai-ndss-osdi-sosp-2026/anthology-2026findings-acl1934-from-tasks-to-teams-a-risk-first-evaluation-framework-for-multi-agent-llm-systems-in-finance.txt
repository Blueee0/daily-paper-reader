## Abstract 

Current financial benchmarks prioritize large language models (LLMs) for task accuracy and portfolio returns, yet overlook risks aris-ing from multi-agent cooperation, tool-sharing, and real-world financial actions. We intro-duce M-SAEA, a Multi-agent, Safety-Aware 

Evaluation Agent that audits LLM teams with-out fine-tuning, deploying ten probes across four layers: model , workflow , interaction , and 

system , to yield a continuous risk vector and natural-language rationale. Evaluated across three high-stakes tasks (finance management, webshop automation, transactional services) with six prominent models, M-SAEA (i) identi-fies unsafe trajectories with minimal false pos-itives, (ii) reveals latent risks (e.g., temporal staleness) that are not addressed by standard metrics, and (iii) provides granular, actionable scores for balancing safety and latency pre-deployment. By quantifying safety as a model-agnostic metric, M-SAEA reorients evaluation from individual tasks to collaborative teams, offering a robust template for risk-first assess-ment of agentic AI in finance and beyond. 

## 1 Introduction 

Large language models (LLMs) are rapidly evolv-ing from single-turn assistants into collabora-tive agents that share information, call external tools, and together underpin automated trading desks, portfolio rebalancers, and risk-analytics pipelines (Ullah et al., 2024; Xiao et al., 2025). When several such agents cooperate, small local errors can cascade, amplifying financial loss far be-yond what any one model could cause in isolation. Yet today’s evaluation culture still revolves around benchmark accuracy and return metrics, treating each agent as an independent learner and ignoring the hazards that emerge only when agents interact. Existing benchmarks typically focus on task-specific metrics such as accuracy, F1 score, or ROUGE (Xie et al., 2024a), which capture finan-cial performance but ignore the unique safety chal-lenges of finance. Unlike many standard LLM settings, financial systems are highly fragile and uncertain, where even minor errors can lead to cas-cading failures and substantial losses (Desai et al., 2024; Li et al., 2024b). The safety of LLM agents extends beyond their ability to generate accurate outputs; it includes their resilience in navigating the intricacies and dynamics of financial systems (Ullah et al., 2024; Boi et al., 2024). A model that appears “success-ful” under conventional benchmarks may still fail in real-world scenarios, incurring systemic con-sequences (McIntosh et al., 2024). For exam-ple, the Freysa AI agent lost $47,000 due to a se-curity vulnerability, where users exploited attack prompts to manipulate the model into bypassing security checks and executing unauthorized trans-actions (Park, 2024). Similarly, a user lost $2,500 

due to GPT-generated (Achiam et al., 2023) phish-ing content, which recommended a fraudulent site while writing a transaction bot (Mitchell, 2024). These cases highlight the immediate and vulnera-ble risks posed by LLM agents in financial domain. To address this, we advocate a risk-first evalua-tion paradigm for multi-agent LLM systems. We introduce M-SAEA, a Multi-agent Safety-

Aware Evaluation Agent that audits entire teams without fine-tuning or model access. In this work we focus on the financial setting: M-SAEA’s probes, tools, and system checks are instantiated with finance-specific language, APIs, and con-straints, and all empirical validation is performed on finance trajectories. M-SAEA issues targeted probes across four layers: 1. Model layer: hallu-cinations, temporal staleness, and over-confidence inside each LLM, 2. ## 2 Related Work 

Language Agents and Applications. Recent ad-vanced progress of Large Language Models has significantly boosted the development of language agents (Sun et al., 2024b; Durante et al., 2024; Feng et al., 2024; Wu et al., 2024a) that could interact with environments to perform complex tasks. Methods such as prompt engineering (Yao et al., 2022b), tool or code use (Ma et al., 2024; Sun et al., 2024a), self-improvement (Hu and Shu, 2023; Cheng et al., 2024a), multi-model collabora-tion (Wu et al., 2023; Sun et al., 2023; Jiang et al., 2024; Li et al., 2023; Jin et al., 2024) or finetuning with trajectories (Cheng et al., 2024b; Wu et al., 2024b; Xie et al., 2024b; Chen et al., 2024) has enabled impressive performances on a wide range of real-world tasks that are involved with web (Yao et al., 2022a; Deng et al., 2024), desktop (Kapoor et al., 2025; Niu et al., 2024) and mobile platforms (Li et al., 2024c; Wang et al., 2024). With these success in general tasks, language agents are also recently applied into financial domain such as fi-nancial question answering (Fatemi and Hu, 2024), financial decision making (Ding et al., 2024; Yang et al., 2024; Li et al., 2024a; Yu et al., 2024), and financial simulation (Gao et al., 2024b). In this work, we will focus on this nuanced application of language agents in financial domain. 

Evaluation Metrics in Financial Benchmarks. 

Existing financial benchmarks primarily focus on task performance, such as accuracy and perfor-mance (Xie et al., 2024a; Yuan et al., 2024; Is-lam et al., 2023; Zhang et al., 2025; Bigeard et al., 2025), which may not be sufficient to capture the real-world financial risks. The primary evaluation metrics can be categorized into two groups: (1) accuracy-based metrics, and (2) investment per-formance metrics. The accuracy-based metrics in-clude F1 score, precision, recall, BLEU, ROUGE, METEOR, MSE and MAE (Zhang et al., 2024b; Hirano, 2024; Quan and Liu, 2024; Li et al., 2024d). The investment performance metrics include An-nualized Rate of Return (ARR), Annualized Ex-cess Rate of Return (AERR), Annualized Volatility (ANVOL), Sharpe Ratio (SR), Maximum Draw-down (MD), Calmar Ratio (CR), Maximum Draw-down Duration (MDD), Annualized Volatility (AV), Sortino Ratio (SoR), Win Rate (WR) (Li et al., 2024a; Yu et al., 2024; Li et al., 2024d; Chen et al., 2025; Patwardhan et al., 2025). While these met-rics are useful for basic performance assessment, they fail to account for higher-order risks concerns, which are crucial in real-world financial applica-38820 tions. ## 3 Safety Challenges Specific to Multi-Agent System 

High-stakes financial systems require stringent demands on reliability, accuracy, and robust-ness—qualities that are not always guaranteed by current LLMs (Nie et al., 2024). We categorize the risks associated with LLMs in finance into two classes: intrinsic challenges that originate from the models themselves (Section 3.1) and external chal-lenges that arise from the interaction of LLMs with external systems and workflows (Section 3.2). 

3.1 Intrinsic Risks from LLM Agents 

LLMs generate outputs based on patterns learned from large-scale text corpora (Naveed et al., 2023; Zhao et al., 2023; Liu et al., 2024). While this results in fluent language, it can also lead to mean-ingful errors (Marchisio et al., 2024). In casual usage like chatbots, these mistakes might not mat-ter much. However, in finance, even small errors can lead to serious consequences, including finan-cial losses, legal risks, and damage to reputation. 

Illusory Confidence In finance, decision-making hinges on uncertainty quantification and risk as-sessment (Fadina et al., 2024), as formalized in modern portfolio theory (Elton et al., 2009) and utility theory (Stigler, 1950), which demand pre-cise confidence intervals for robust strategies. Yet, LLMs often exhibit unwarranted certainty in out-puts, irrespective of factual accuracy (Tanneru et al., 2024; Xiong et al., 2024), clashing with probabilis-tic risk principles (Bedford and Cooke, 2001). This overconfidence can misguide market trend analy-ses, leading to portfolio misallocation and eroded risk-adjusted returns. 

Hallucination LLMs’ hallucination poses a fun-damental challenge to the integrity of financial ana-lytics (Kang and Liu, 2023; Roychowdhury et al., 2023). Although minor hallucinations may appear harmless in casual scenarios, they can have severe consequences in finance. For instance, fabricating earnings data can lead to false signals that influ-ence the algorithmic trading systems and human decision-making. Given that finance is predicated on accurate information, hallucinations may lead to financial losses and legal liabilities. 

Lack of Temporal Awareness Adapting to the dynamic nature of financial markets, as empha-sized by dynamic portfolio optimization (Nystrup et al., 2018), remains a challenge for LLMs trained on static corpora lacking real-time knowledge up-dates. This temporal misalignment risks reliance on outdated economic or regulatory data, poten-tially skewing analyses—e.g., an LLM might over-look recent central bank policy shifts, yielding erro-neous trading recommendations. In time-sensitive markets, such limitations critically undermine the reliability of LLM-driven financial systems. 

Poor Handling of Domain-Specific Reasoning 

Financial decision-making hinges on interpreting domain-specific language (Ke et al., 2025), com-plex contracts (Lai et al., 2024), and regulatory texts (Cao and Feinstein, 2024). LLMs, trained on general corpora, often falter in distinguishing nu-anced terminologies, risking errors in specialized reasoning. For instance, misinterpreting smart con-tract intent in blockchain transactions (Ressi et al., 2024) can lead to misleading decisions, particularly where precise semantic grasp is critical. 

Adversarial Vulnerabilities Adversaries exploit LLM agents via crafted prompts, manipulating out-puts to skew market analyses (Xu et al., 2024; Schulhoff et al., 2023). This vulnerability is critical in trading systems, where biased insights can dis-tort strategies and risk assessments. Autonomous agents amplify these risks through cascading er-rors in multi-step workflows (Hong et al., 2024), as manipulated inputs propagate, magnifying initial inaccuracies into systemic failures. 

Dependency on Prompt Design Users of LLM agents in financial domain may encounter variabil-ity in the outputs depending on how prompts are phrased (Zhao et al., 2024b). The unpredictabil-ity complicates their practical use, particularly in tasks requiring consistent and reliable results. For instance, slight changes in how a user phrases a risk assessment query can lead to different outputs, even when the task remains the same (Yu, 2023). This inconsistency poses a challenge for users who rely on agents for critical decision-making. In au-tomated trading pipelines, such variability can lead to inefficiencies, errors, or missed opportunities. 

Lack of Interpretability The risk management frameworks require transparency and accountabil-ity in decision-making processes (Schuett, 2024). However, an LLM’s “black-box” nature makes it 38821 challenging to explain how it derives its outputs. Regulators and institutional investors emphasize the need for explainable models to ensure traceabil-ity and compliance. The inability to clarify how LLMs produce their outputs creates challenges for adoption in high-stakes domains, where decision-making processes must be fully understood and auditable to users (Hung et al., 2023). 

Limitations in Multimodal Integration Finan-cial decision-making requires integrating multi-modal data, including textual analysis, numeri-cal computations, and visual elements such as stock charts (Zhang et al., 2024a). Decision sci-ence emphasizes processing diverse information in dynamic environments (Schleibaum et al., 2024). However, current LLMs are primarily text-focused and often fail to effectively fuse textual, numerical, and visual data. This limitation is particularly evi-dent in quantitative research and algorithmic trad-ing, where understanding relationships across data types is crucial, thereby reducing the analytical po-tential of LLM agents and limiting their provision of actionable financial insights. 

3.2 Risks from External Interactions API/Tool Dependency LLM agents frequently rely on external APIs and tools (Shen et al., 2025). For example, to retrieve live market data, execute trades, or query regulatory information. While such integrations expand a LLM’s capabilities, they also involve dependencies on systems that can be error-prone (Zhao et al., 2024a). A malfunctioning data feed might provide inaccurate prices (Tivnan et al., 2018), or a compromised API could intentionally inject misleading content (Zhao et al., 2024a). Be-cause the LLM treats these external tools as sup-plementary knowledge sources, any errors or ma-nipulations can propagate unchecked. 

Multi-Step Tasks Financial operations often in-volve complex, multi-step workflows: evaluating a company’s fundamentals, performing sentiment analysis on news, applying risk models, and ulti-mately placing trades (Nie et al., 2024). Small er-rors at any step in these workflows may accumulate into larger failures. For example, an incorrect news interpretation might lead to a flawed risk assess-ment, which in turn could trigger an inappropriate trade. Unlike single-turn tasks that can be manu-ally reviewed, multi-step tasks can magnify small inaccuracies, culminating in decisions that carry substantial monetary risks. 

3.3 Risks from Inter-Agent Communication Cross-Agent Cascade Risk When two or more LLM agents collaborate, each agent tends to treat every peer message as ground-truth context (Tran et al., 2025). This “optimistic trust” means that a single mis-parsed quote, spoofed instruction, or prompt-level jailbreak can ripple across the agent team, triggering uncontrolled leverage loops before any human can intervene. Agents act in parallel and share tools (e.g., the same trading API), race con-ditions and resource-locking failures become real: one agent may cancel an order at the exact millisec-ond another submits it, or overwrite a risk cap the treasury bot just set (Gao et al., 2024a). Attackers can exploit these gaps by injecting conflicting goals (“buy” vs “hedge”) or replaying stale messages, forcing agents into contradiction and inflating sys-temic exposure. Without explicit consensus checks, versioned state, and cryptographic identity, inter-agent communication becomes a first-class source of financial risk, one invisible to benchmarks that evaluate agents in isolation (Li et al., 2024e).

## 6 Experiment Results 

Experiment Setup Trajectories and models. To isolate the audit-ing behavior of M-SAEA from any trajectory-generation choices, we do not regenerate agent trajectories. Instead we audit existing, expert-validated multi-step financial trajectories drawn from the finance-domain portion of R-Judge (Yuan et al., 2024), which to our knowledge is the only publicly available benchmark where multi-step agent trajectories in finance carry human-expert 

SAFE /UNSAFE labels. Concretely, we use 126 R-Judge finance trajectories plus 9 additional bor-derline instances we manually constructed in the same style to cover corner cases, for 135 trajecto-ries in total, grouped into three task clusters: (1) 

Finance Management (cryptocurrency use-cases such as Bitcoin, Ethereum, Binance), (2) Web-shop Automation (online shop and Shopify inte-grations), and (3) Transactional Services (bank and PayPal scenarios). We instantiate M-SAEA’s eval-uator LLM E with six backbone models—two API-based: GPT-4o (Achiam et al., 2023) and Claude-3.5-Sonnet (Anthropic); and four open-weights: Qwen3-235B (Yang et al., 2025), Llama-3.3-70B, Llama-3.1-8B (Dubey et al., 2024), and DeepSeek-R1 (Guo et al., 2025)—yielding 135 ×6×10 = 8,100 (trajectory, model, probe) triplets and cor-responding natural-language rationales. 

Protocol. For each trajectory M-SAEA emits the ten-dimensional risk vector, the normalized risk score S (Eq. 3), and the binary verdict ˆy (Eq. 4). Unless stated otherwise, we use the conservative default threshold θ = 0 .20 : any trajectory with 

S > θ is flagged UNSAFE ; a θ-sensitivity analysis is reported in Section 6.3. To ensure comparability, all evaluator backbones see the same prompts, sam-pling parameters, and trajectories, and all severity scores are standardized to a 0–100 scale.

6.3 Per-Model Baseline and Threshold Sensitivity Per-model baseline. Because no established framework specifically targets financial risk in multi-agent LLM systems (Table 1), we build a per-model baseline under R-Judge’s own protocol: for each backbone, we measure its Original Accu-racy (single-shot R-Judge judgment) and its accu-racy when wrapped by M-SAEA’s ten-probe audit-ing. Across all six backbones, routing the decision through M-SAEA improves accuracy by 19–45 absolute points (Table 5a), with the largest gains on the alignment-tuned API models (GPT-4o: +39 .6,Claude-3.5-Sonnet: +44 .9). The improvement is not driven by any single backbone: the structured, multi-probe auditing layer is what closes the gap to the human-expert labels, positioning M-SAEA as a post-hoc auditing wrapper over existing pipelines rather than a replacement. 

Threshold sensitivity. The verdict rule in Eq. (4) is gated by θ, which we treat as an explicit risk-tolerance hyperparameter. Varying θ on the Finance-Management cluster (Table 5b) shows ac-curacy is not tied to a single value: it remains high across θ ∈ [0 .10 , 0.20] and peaks at θ=0 .15 . Be-cause financial tasks are heterogeneous, we do not claim θ=0 .20 is universally optimal; we adopt it as a conservative default , with lower θ trading util-ity for lower residual risk and higher θ admitting more aggressive actions. In deployment, θ can be calibrated from historical incident data, expert judgment, or regulatory risk appetite.

6.4 Human Study 

To check whether M-SAEA’s verdicts and ratio-nales align with human intuition beyond aggregate accuracy, we ran a pilot study: three annotators (1 MS, 2 PhD, all familiar with agentic LLMs) inde-pendently reviewed 20 trajectories sampled from our finance set. For each, they saw the task, the full agent–tool trace, and M-SAEA’s SAFE /UNSAFE 

verdict with a short rationale, and rated each item on three 1–5 Likert scales: 

Correctness: does the verdict match theirs for this trajectory? 

Reasonableness: is the rationale a faithful justifi-cation given the trace? 

Conciseness: is the rationale clear and brief? On these 20 trajectories, M-SAEA agrees with the human-majority SAFE /UNSAFE label in ∼ 90% 

of cases, consistent with its aggregate accuracy in Table 5a. Annotators judged most rationales rea-sonable and correct, with only a small fraction mis-leading or verbose. Though small by design, the combination of high Likert scores and high label agreement mitigates circular-reasoning concerns and indicates that M-SAEA’s judgments track hu-man intuition on realistic financial traces. 

## 7 Conclusion 

M-SAEA transforms abstract safety concerns into measurable quantities by shadowing every mes-sage, tool call, and inter-agent exchange to deliver a ten-axis risk vector that correlates tightly with ground-truth SAFE /UNSAFE labels. The framework exposes overlooked failure modes without model fine-tuning or task-specific heuristics. Practitioners can proactively de-risk prompts, tools, or rate limits before incidents occur. By grounding safety in ac-tionable metrics, M-SAEA charts a path to agentic AI that is both powerful and provably reliable. 38827 Limitations 

Our study prioritizes interpretability and repro-ducibility over breadth. Empirical validation is lim-ited to the financial domain : all probes, tools, and experiments are instantiated with finance-specific language, APIs, and constraints, and we do not make empirical claims beyond finance. The four-layer taxonomy is conceptually domain-agnostic, but transferring M-SAEA to areas such as health-care, legal reasoning, or robotics would require domain-specific probes and tools and is left to future work. The multi-axis risk scores are in-tended as comparative signals; mapping them to institution-specific loss tolerances, and the choice of aggregation weights w and threshold θ, depend on the risk appetite of the deploying institution. We use an LLM-based evaluator for consistency and cost—standardized prompts, fixed sampling pa-rameters, and our human study (Section 6.4) help mitigate evaluator variance, but a larger-scale eval-uator robustness study with more annotators and more models is left to future work. Finally, while scenarios exercise realistic APIs, production de-ployments add operational controls; we position our harness as a conservative auditing baseline that complements live integration testing.
